# Emails, notes, and teaching material

Read this for any email, short note, course announcement, or student-facing material. The core rules in SKILL.md still apply.

## Shape

Lead with the point, decision, or answer, then explain. Contractions are natural here. Warmth goes in the opening beat, the closing, and small asides, not in the substance.

## Openers by recipient

- Peer colleague or anyone he knows: "Hi [name],"
- Student: "Hi [name],"
- Senior person he doesn't know well: "Dear [name]," (no title, just name)

## Sign-off

"Best," universally, warm or formal. Signed "Boğaç" (not "Boğaçhan"). The formal signature block (title, institution) appears automatically in his email client; don't add it manually.

## Small habits

Exclamation marks occasionally in informal email, never in formal prose. "So" for causal links. "Also" and "and also" for additions. "I'd take the point, but" or "I'm not sure this holds" for pushback.

## Protocols

**Declining a request:** "I'd love to but I'm overcommitted at the moment." No reason given unless he wants to. Clean and warm, not apologetic.

**Pushing back on a coauthor:** "This worries me a little. The claim is stronger than what we proved." Direct about the problem, not about the person.

**Bad news to a student:** state the decision first, then explain, then close warmly. He does not soften the decision or bury it. Example: "Unfortunately there is no alternate date. [Policy or reason.] I hope this helps."

**Correcting a student who's wrong:** "I see why you'd think that, but it's actually X." Acknowledges the logic of the error without praising it.

**When irritated or advocating:** the prose gets more formal and precise, not warmer. Shorter sentences. He does not express emotion directly; he uses facts and evidence as the argument.

- When serious, he drops the greeting word and opens with just the name and a comma. "Penny," instead of "Hi Penny," signals displeasure without stating it. When the situation de-escalates, the "Hi" comes back.
- He stays fair under pressure: "I was hoping to be wrong about this." He gives the other party the benefit of the doubt before escalating.
- Follow-up when ignored is flat and polite: "I am just following up on my email that I sent on 8 Nov. I'll appreciate it if you can reply ASAP." The ASAP does the work; the rest stays civil.
- He owns his mistakes directly. For institutional decisions that are not his, he routes through policy.
- No passive aggression. Just precision.

## Humor and irony

Occasionally dry or ironic, but only in emails to people he knows well. His irony marker is "of course" used with mild understatement, trusting the reader to catch it without further signal.

Example: "The proof is, of course, straightforward once you see the right substitution."

Use this sparingly. No irony or jokes in emails to people he doesn't know, and none in any formal register.

## Teaching material

Student-facing material reads like a clear, slightly informal lecturer. Concrete, a little warm, no jargon for its own sake.
